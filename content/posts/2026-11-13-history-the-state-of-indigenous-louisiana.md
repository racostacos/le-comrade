title: The State of Indigenous Louisiana
section: history
summary: "Before Louisiana was Louisiana, the Quinipissa and other Indigenous peoples already called this land home. French colonial accounts reveal a history of coercion, violence, disease, and enslavement that challenges the familiar story of Louisiana's founding."
date: 2026-11-13
published: true
---
La Salle and his men reached the Mississippi River Delta on April 6, 1682 where they sailed “till the 6th, when we discovered three channels” (La Metairie, 1682/1905, p.165). Meanwhile on the West Bank of the Mississippi near present-day Hahnville, the Quinipissa already had established settlements in the area which we know from French observations (Hastings, 2009; Membre, 1682/1905, pp. 143-149). The Quinipissa are classified by historical ethnographiers as a Muskogean-speaking people, however their language was ignored and undocumented by Europeans (Swanton, 1911; Hastings, 2009). La Salle’s expedition reported an arrow fired towards their group, in which the French did not return fire (Swanton, 1911, pp. 279-280). In April 1682, Henri de Tonti approached the Quinipissas settlement again. He describes “taking four of the women, encamped on the opposite bank” , with one captive sent back carrying French merchandise (Tonti, 1693/1905, p.28). The Frenchmen used coercion in order to make trade with the Quinipissas, which unsurprisingly lead to “the next morning, at daybreak, the scoundrels attacked us”. Just the night before, Father Zenobius Membre describes the two groups exchanging hostages, concluding peace, and even participating in a feast. The next day, Tonti burned their canoes and only decided not to attack their settlement due to concerns about their ammunition supply (Tonti, 1693/1905, p.28). When Tonti returned in Spring 1686, he recorded “the chiefs brought me the calumet”. He stated the chiefs expressed regret over the earlier encounter by giving him a pipe (Tonti, 1693/1905, p.35). By Iberville’s expedition in March 1699, the Quinipissa were no longer at their former village (Inter-Tribal Council of Louisiana, n.d., “Quinipissa” section). Campanella writes that “fully one-quarter of the Bayogoula in present-day Iberville Parish had recently perished of smallpox” (Campanella, 2022). The Bayogoula people lived alongside the Quinipissa and the Mugalasha farther upriver. Harrell suspects the Quinipissa integrated into the Mugulasha, but this has not been genetically substantiated. If this is true, we can assume from Campanella that the group was decimated by smallpox by the return of Iberville. Just 27 years later in 1726, Louisiana census recorded 229 enslaved Indigenous people alongside 1,540 enslaved Africans (Harrell, 2023). As soon as the French arrived on Indigenous land in present-day Louisiana, they coerced them to trade, gave them terminal illnesses, and forced them into bondage.
