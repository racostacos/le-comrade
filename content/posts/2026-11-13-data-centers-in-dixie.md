Our local leadership has not demonstrated that it is prepared to represent the interests and well-being of the People when a corporation arrives waving billions of dollars. Livingston Parish should impose an **indefinite moratorium on data center development**. Residents deserve to know what they are being asked to surrender, what they are guaranteed in return, and who pays when the promises fall short.

We all see what’s going on in Richland Parish, where Mark Zuckerberg’s Meta is building its largest data center. By July 2026, Meta’s announced investment had grown to more than $50 billion, with 7,500 jobs at peak construction and 1,000 operational roles promised afterward (Meta, 2026a). Jobs. Investment. New horizons. We’ve heard this one before. Let’s talk about those new horizons, shall we?

Meta celebrates teacher bonuses of roughly $50,000, funded by increased local tax revenue associated with the project. Its own announcement describes annual supplemental payments, including a smaller payment the previous year (Meta, 2026b). Teachers deserve every penny. But what will schools receive ten or twenty years from now? Where is the independently reviewed accounting of recurring revenue, tax concessions, public-service costs, and long-term liabilities? A photograph of a teacher holding a check cannot answer those questions.

And those jobs? Meta’s own figures distinguish 7,500 **peak construction jobs** from 1,000 **operational roles**. Using the announced figures, that is approximately $50 million in private investment per operational role. This ratio measures private capital spending relative to the promised permanent workforce (Meta, 2026a). How many positions will go to people already living in Richland Parish? What wages will they earn? What training will make those jobs accessible to residents? Where are the guarantees for the people who already call this parish home?

Independent research gives us good reason to demand more than a corporate announcement. An updated Brookings study examining approximately 1,500 data centers found that they do create local jobs, but that comparisons ignoring communities’ existing growth trends exaggerate their employment effects. The researchers found no wage increase attributable to the developments, while home prices increased by approximately 2–5%. These broader findings challenge the assumption that a huge investment guarantees better living standards for everyone nearby. Meta’s unusually large campus still requires its own assessment (Bahar & Wright, 2026).

When advocates sought evidence behind Meta’s promises, regulators helped keep it out of reach. In August 2026, the Louisiana Public Service Commission voted 3–1 to overturn an administrative law judge’s subpoena seeking information about permanent jobs and electricity demand. Commissioner Jean-Paul Coussan argued that the detailed information was unnecessary for the commission’s role. Commissioner Davante Lewis dissented, warning about power infrastructure that could outlast Meta’s contractual commitments (Crockett, 2026). **Apparently, the promises are good enough to promote, but the evidence behind them is too much to demand.**

Entergy says its agreements with Meta will produce approximately $2.65 billion in customer benefits over twenty years. Its SEC filing also describes contractual protections, including minimum bills, early-termination obligations, and a Meta parent-company guarantee (Entergy, 2026a, 2026b). Those protections matter. So does examining their limits. What happens if electricity demand falls, the project changes, or infrastructure remains after the contracts end? Residents should not have to accept a savings forecast on faith while fighting to obtain the information needed to test it.

The same cloudy atmosphere surrounds Livingston Parish. At the November 3, 2025, zoning commission meeting, Councilman Ricky Goff acknowledged signing a confidentiality agreement concerning the Buddy Ellis Road property and indicated that the entire council was not aware of the project. On November 20, the council unanimously approved rezoning approximately 692.6 acres from residential and light-commercial classifications to I-2 heavy industrial (Thornton, 2026). One elected official supported a major land-use change while holding information about the project that had not been shared with the entire council. That alone deserves scrutiny. Should residents be comfortable with a project simply because he is?

On September 1, 2026, Parish President Randy Delatte denied signing an NDA and said there was no data center plan to announce. His statement also said that, to his knowledge, no locally elected official had signed one concerning a business coming to the parish (WBRZ Staff, 2026). That assurance deserves an explanation alongside Goff’s publicly reported acknowledgment. By October 1, Delatte was saying he expected a data center within five to ten years, while maintaining that no project had been finalized (Combs, 2026). Residents are entitled to a coherent account of what officials know, what they are pursuing, and what commitments they have made.

Between those statements, the council adopted **Ordinance No. 26-29**, regulating power plants, requiring special-use permits, and restricting them to I-2 heavy-industrial zoning (Arceneaux, 2026; Livingston Parish Council, 2026). As introduced, its standards included at least ten contiguous acres, a 500-foot separation between plant structures and residences, a 60-decibel noise ceiling, and a permit fee of $100 per acre with a $10,000 minimum (Collins, 2026). These are safeguards worth having, but are they enough? The ordinance gives officials a permitting process. **Residents deserve evidence that this process will protect them from what officials permit.**

Take the noise limit. The World Health Organization recommends an annual average nighttime noise target of 40 decibels outside bedrooms to protect health, including sleep. Its 55-decibel interim target is explicitly less protective, particularly for vulnerable people (World Health Organization Regional Office for Europe, 2009). Those measurements cannot be directly equated with the parish’s reported 60-decibel ceiling without knowing where, how, and over what period sound is measured. **That is precisely the problem with treating “60 decibels” as an assurance of safety.** Where is the requirement demonstrating that a plant operating within that ceiling will still let its neighbors sleep?

We already have evidence that complying with a noise ordinance does not necessarily protect residents’ quality of life. Virginia’s legislative watchdog found that some data centers produce persistent low-frequency noise that rarely violates existing ordinances, yet nearby residents report harm to their well-being. It recommended better measurement methods and enforceable zoning restrictions addressing that noise specifically (Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission [JLARC], 2024). Imagine being told the machinery keeping you miserable is perfectly legal. Livingston should demand evidence that its rules prevent that outcome before approving the source of the problem.

And 500 feet? What evidence establishes that a particular power plant is safe at that distance? EPA evaluates downwind pollution using emissions and weather conditions (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.). A ten-acre minimum tells us how much land a developer needs. What will neighboring households breathe or hear? Calling the property “heavy industrial” does not move the homes already nearby. Virginia’s review specifically found that placing industrial zoning beside residential areas, and approving rezonings near sensitive locations, contributed to adverse residential impacts (JLARC, 2024). **Changing the label on the map does not change who lives next door.**

Then there is the fee. Under the proposed schedule, a 100-acre power plant would owe the same $10,000 minimum as a ten-acre plant (Collins, 2026). That calculation exposes how little the fee tells residents about protection: acreage alone does not measure generating capacity, emissions, monitoring needs, or potential damage. How will those costs be covered? **Before officials congratulate themselves on collecting the fee, show us the independently calculated costs, who must pay them, and the enforceable funding that protects residents if the operator fails.**

These rules regulate power-generating facilities. A data center campus and its supporting infrastructure require a comprehensive assessment of their own. Residents should not have to wait until an approved facility becomes a problem to discover that the standards were too weak. That is why Livingston needs a moratorium while these questions are answered.

Louisiana is no stranger to inadequate industrial safeguards. An October 2024 Legislative Auditor report estimated that addressing the state’s existing orphaned oil and gas wells would cost **$542.9 million**. Required financial security remained insufficient to cover plugging costs. The state restoration program relies on industry fees, with federal grants also funding cleanup (Louisiana Legislative Auditor, 2024). There is the lesson in black and white: the state allowed industrial liabilities to grow beyond the money secured to address them. Why should we accept another development model without demanding that the full obligations be funded and enforceable from the beginning?

What about the canals? The U.S. Geological Survey identifies canals constructed for oil and gas production as a major contributor to Louisiana’s wetland loss (U.S. Geological Survey, 2024). The damage is documented. The warning should be obvious: an industry’s investment figures do not tell us what it will leave behind.

That is why Livingston Parish needs an indefinite moratorium, with no automatic expiration that treats the passage of time as proof of safety. Before any reconsideration, residents should demand published agreements, independent assessments of water and drainage impacts, enforceable noise limits, transparent utility-cost protections, audited employment commitments, and financial security for closure and cleanup. Our officials owe us that accountability.

Louisiana does not owe any company its land, tax concessions, or public trust simply because of an enormous investment figure. Officials should have to demonstrate that residents will be better off after construction ends. **We should not have to prove that a project will ruin our lives before our government requires the developer to prove its claims.**

Livingston Parish residents must work together to demand this moratorium. Contact your council member. Attend the hearings. Ask for the agreements and the numbers. Support the effort to stop approvals until the People can make an informed decision. Our homes and communities are worth more than a promise that someone else is comfortable making.
